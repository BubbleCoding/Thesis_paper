\label{sec:Evaluation}
This chapter evaluates the prototype from both a user and a designer perspective. First, a user case study was conducted in which four Dungeons \& Dragons sessions were recorded, turned into comics, and then evaluated by the players through a questionnaire. Their responses give qualitative insight into the feasibility of AI retelling, the current quality of the system, and the ethics of AI retelling. Second, a self-observation and data analysis examined each stage of the pipeline, from transcription to image generation, to see where errors appear and how they shape the final output. Taken together, these two perspectives provide a comprehensive view: the user study shows how the results are received in practice, while the self-analysis reveals the technical causes behind those impressions. The main goal is to draw out design requirements, with a secondary aim of assessing current performance, both of which guide how the system can be developed into a more coherent, meaningful, and ethically sound tool for retelling tabletop sessions.

\section{User Case Study}

The user case study involved four Dungeons \& Dragons 5th edition sessions which were recorded and then processed through the artifact to generate a comic book. The players then received the resulting comic book and were asked to complete a questionnaire. Each session followed the same story, was run in the same location, and used the same audio equipment. The first three sessions were run by the same Dungeon Master, while the fourth session was led by another. For detailed explanations of the methodology see Section~\ref{sec:DataCollection}. In this section, the results of the case study are discussed and the questionnaire is presented in full. For all the detailed results of the questionnaire see Appendix~\ref{app:questionnaire-results}. First the questionnaire as presented to the participants is shown, then the results are discussed.  

The questionnaire was sent to all ten participants, of whom five completed it. While this response rate may appear modest, the five responses provided a great deal of qualitative richness. Because many of the questions were framed in a way that encouraged reflection and open-ended feedback, even a small set of responses was sufficient to surface meaningful insights into authorship, coherence, and ethics. One advantage of this smaller dataset is that it allowed for detailed, question-by-question analysis rather than broad codification, giving space for the individual voices of participants to come through. The results should therefore be read not as a statistical generalisation but as an exploratory dataset that highlights key challenges, user perspectives, and design opportunities for future iterations of the system.

\subsection*{The Questionnaire}
This section shows the full questionnaire as presented to the participants. The questionnaire was anonymous, but participants received my email address and phone number if they wanted to contact me for follow-up questions or anything else. The questionnaire is used to evaluate the artifact on the design requirements and to gather feedback on the artifact. 
The questionnaire is divided into 4 sections: Background; Exposition, Roleplay and Rule interaction; Ethical Considerations; Your Experience of the Comic. The \textit{background} section is used as a control round to see if they actually did read the comic book and if they did participate, furthermore it is asked if they are familiar with comic books. This is done with the idea that there experience of comics might impact someone's ideas of the results. The second section asks questions about \textit{the three primary forms of player interaction} as discussed in section~\ref{sec:2.6}. The third section \textit{Ethical Considerations} is mainly a person point of interest as I am curious to know what people think of creating comic books with AI, and through that in the larger context of generative AI in general. However it is also something that is a key part of the creation of this tool as having an ethically sound project is important. The last section \textit{Your Experience of the Comic} focuses on the participant and what their ideas about the results are. It looks at the coherence between the comic book and the original session. Giving the participant a lot of freedom to answer broader questions in whatever way they want. This section is placed at the end, because this way the participant has already been thinking about the results for some time and can now distil their thoughts into text. 

All the questions have been made without the usage of example questions or a framework. As this is a very specific topic to ask questions about and because I have academic experience with creating questionnaires it should provide valuable information. In total there are 19 questions with 2 sub questions: 4 are Yes/No questions; 8 are Likert scale questions; 7 are open ended questions; and 1 is a box select question. Furthermore each section is accompanied by a short explanatory introduction so that the participant has some context as to why they are answering these questions. Even though more questions could have been asked, keeping the total number low is an effective way to maintain participant engagement and improve the overall quality of responses \cite{sharma2022short}. If the final section had been the 40th or 50th question, participants would likely have experienced questionnaire fatigue, leading to less thoughtful and less useful answers. The goal was to establish a baseline of interest and contemplation early on, and then position the most important questions toward the end of the survey, when respondents were still attentive and a bit more invested in the topic.

\subsubsection*{Exposition, Roleplay and Rule interaction}
In tabletop role-playing games, three main activities usually happen:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Exposition}: the Game Master sets the scene and describes the world.
    \item \textbf{Roleplay}: players act and speak in character, interacting with each other and NPCs.
    \item \textbf{Rule interaction}: players roll dice, make checks, and use mechanics to resolve actions.
\end{itemize}
This section asks about how these different aspects of play were represented in the comic.

\begin{enumerate}
    \setcounter{enumi}{3}
    \item To what extent do you feel that the Game Master’s exposition is present in the comic book? \hfill Likert scale (Completely -- Not at all)
    \item To what extent do you feel that your in-character roleplay actions are conveyed in the comic book? \hfill Likert scale (Completely -- Not at all)
    \item To what extent do you feel that your actions as a player (choices, decisions, initiatives) are conveyed in the comic book? \hfill Likert scale (Completely -- Not at all)
    \item Did you notice any text or visuals in the comic that explicitly referenced game rules (e.g., dice rolls, checks, modifiers)? \hfill Box select 0/1-5/5-10/10-15/15+
    \item Would you like rule interactions to be part of the comic book? \hfill Yes / No
    \item Of the three activities (exposition, roleplay, rule interaction), which do you feel was represented best in the comic? Why? \hfill Open-ended
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Results}
This section discusses the responses to the questionnaire. Each of the four sections is considered as a whole, with particular attention paid to results that reveal interesting patterns or insights.

\subsubsection*{Background}
The first section serves primarily as a check and does not offer substantial analytical insights. However, one interesting detail is that a participant reported not having read the comic book, yet proceeded to answer subsequent questions in ways that suggest they had. It is likely that this was an input error and that the participant intended to select “yes.”

\subsubsection*{Exposition, Roleplay and Rule interaction}
The results in this section are somewhat limited in scientific strength due to the small sample size and the distribution of responses. Nonetheless, several patterns can be identified. 

Questions \questionref{question 4}, \questionref{question 5}, and \questionref{question 6} cluster around the middle of the Likert scale, with means of 2.8, 2.6, and 2.8, respectively. Notably, no participants selected the extreme categories (“completely” or “not at all”). This signals that there is potential for the system as the participants consistently observed some degree of exposition and roleplay. However it also shows that there is still a lot of space to grow for the system to become something that is truly good. The most important take away from these questions is that the participants do see exposition and role play within the system and that it can thus be extracted from a audio recording. This matters directly to the research question, as it shows the system can extract core play activities from audio, but not yet to the level of a convincing retelling.

The question regarding references to game rules shows that most participants (4 out of 5) noticed between one and five occurrences. While this does not provide an exact count, it indicates that rule-related content was present enough for respondents to register consciously. Question \questionref{question 8} further shows us that most participants would prefer such rule references to be excluded from the comic. Notably one person did say they want rules to be part of the comic book. This suggests that the inclusion of rule mechanics in narrative retellings may be a matter of personal preference.

Finally question \questionref{question 9} was a open question where we can draw the most important points from. In retrospect, the question itself could have been phrased more effectively. Nevertheless, two key take aways can be drawn. Firstly a lot of the text in the comic book is very descriptive which is something one participant said, which from the context feels like something the participant found odd. Secondly the roleplay was rarely coherent. These two points are most likely very intertwined as they both are a result of the text.

Combined these questions show us that that roleplay and exposition is present in the correct comic book and thus that creating a proper retelling of a TTRPG session with AI is possible. There is however a lot of room for improvement in how the exposition and roleplay panels are handled. 

\subsubsection*{Ethical Considerations}
The responses in this section provide insight into how participants perceive authorship, authenticity, and the ethical implications of using AI in this context. 

Question \questionref{question 10} indicates that participants generally still regard the story depicted in the comic as their own. The mean response of 2.2 on the Likert scale (1 Completely - 5 Not at all) suggests that players recognize their actions and decisions reflected in the panels, indicating that the comic preserves a sense of personal authorship and agency.

Responses to questions \questionref{question 11} and \questionref{question 11 sub} further reveal that participants valued the inclusion of potentially inappropriate, offensive, or misaligned elements from the original play session. Rather than viewing these elements negatively, they appreciated their presence as part of the authentic narrative. This suggests that the generated comic does not simply generalize or sanitize events but captures specific aspects of the play session, thereby creating a faithful retelling.

Finally, questions \questionref{question 12} and \questionref{question 13} directly asked participants about the ethical acceptability of using AI in this type of system. As expected, responses varied considerably.However there is one clear throughline: most of the participants said it is ethically acceptable to use AI for this specific use case, even do they normally find the usage of AI for the creation of art unethical. But within this context, where the goal is to retell your own story without any other goals, such as selling the AI generated, they find it acceptable. This is an interesting line we found where the benefits outweigh the ethical aspects. This suggests that the feasibility of AI retelling is not only technical but also socially/ethically viable, aligning with the research questions concern for meaningful retelling.

This suggests that player perception of authorship and ethical boundaries is closely tied to the notion of personal narrative ownership, an insight that is central to understanding the role of AI in retelling TTRPG experiences

\subsubsection*{Your Experience of the Comic}
This section explores how participants experienced the comic in relation to their original play session, with the aim of identifying design requirements for a retelling system.

Question \questionref{question 14} suggests that the panels captured the overall feeling and atmosphere of the session reasonably well. Participants reported that the panels reflected the essence of the session, although variability in art style, character depictions, and location artwork reduced the perceived quality of the retelling.

Question \questionref{question 20}, the final question, asked participants broadly about improvements they would like to see in the system. Three main points emerged from their responses.

First, several participants noted that some events in the comic are out of chronological order compared to the session. Late-session events occasionally appear early, and some sequences are interleaved or mixed. Participants also described the overall structure as chaotic. This can also be seen in the results of Question \questionref{question 16}, where most people said it was either hard or very hard to follow the story. There are few relations between panels and between text and image. This is primarily attributed to the fact that the character art is inconsistent, as every time the character is represented they look different. The same can be said for the consistency of locations and the art style in general. Together these observations suggest errors in how narrative segments were assembled and ordered. This seems to indicate that most likely the adaptive sliding context system made errors or that the order of transcription was mixed.

Second, one participant mentioned in Question \questionref{question 20} that they would like to see a clear distinction between in-game and out-of-game dialogue. Currently, these two types of dialogue often mix, and as seen in the results of Question \questionref{question 18}, this blending reduces the perceived quality of the retelling. This feedback points to two requirements: out-of-game dialogue should be included, as players see value in it, but it needs to be clearly separated and identifiable. This is further supported by a response to Question \questionref{question 15}, where one participant said they would find it interesting to see important real-life moments included in the comic. For example when players discuss their next moves or collaborate on plans.

Third, one participant reported in Question \questionref{question 20} that some dialogue was attributed to the wrong character. Lines spoken by character X were sometimes assigned to character Y. This suggests that errors occurred in the transcription stage or during the mapping of dialogue to characters in the final comic.

To wrap up all the questions, Question \questionref{question 19} is among the most important questions, as it directly addresses the perceived value of the retelling. In general, participants did not consider the current version of the comic to be a valuable retelling of their session. However, they did see significant potential for a system like this to become a meaningful and accurate retelling tool in the future.

\subsection*{Conclusion}
Across all four sections of the questionnaire, several clear design requirements for an AI-driven retelling system emerge. The system must ensure that events are presented in the correct chronological order to preserve narrative coherence and improve readability. Visual consistency across panels, including character design, location depiction, and overall art style is crucial for recognisability and immersion. Dialogue must be correctly attributed to the speaking character, and the system should include a mechanism to distinguish between in-game and out-of-game speech while still incorporating both where appropriate. Rule references and other meta-level elements should either be excluded or presented in a way that suits individual preferences. Finally, the system should aim to maintain the emotional tone and atmosphere of the original session while providing a clear and structured narrative flow. Together, these requirements form a concrete roadmap for improving the quality, usability, clarity, and perceived value of future iterations of the retelling pipeline.

From a more holistic point of view, the questionnaire results demonstrate that the current prototype already succeeds in capturing certain aspects of a play session, such as atmosphere and moments of exposition or roleplay. At the same time, participants highlighted important shortcomings in structure, consistency, and dialogue handling that limit the comic's effectiveness as a retelling. The responses also show that players generally recognise the story as their own and consider the use of AI acceptable in this specific context, provided that the goal is to retell their own experience rather than to produce commercial artwork. These findings suggest that while the present system cannot yet deliver a fully convincing retelling, it points toward a clear direction for development. The potential recognised by participants indicates that, with refinement along the identified design requirements, AI-assisted comics could become a meaningful and ethically acceptable form of retelling TTRPG sessions.

\section{Self observation and Data analysis}
The creation of a comic book from audio with AI involves many moving parts, each of which can be analysed since all intermediate data is saved during processing. We can examine the audio, the transcript, the generated comic book script, the generated prompts, the generated images, the generated text, and finally the comic book as a whole. By analysing each stage, we can identify where the system struggles and where it performs well, which not only informs future improvements but also helps answer the research question. This section begins with an analysis of the transcription stage. The last step however, combining the comic book panels into a comic book will not be discussed as that is a straightforward layout step that does not involve any AI and cannot fail. The comic as a whole however will be discussed, but during the image generation step.

The analysis in this chapter is done by looking at the data in a holistic manner: scrolling through everything; reading everything; and making notes on everything, using word searches, counting occurrences of specific words, and looking at distributions of various metrics. Finally ChatGPT-5.0 is consulted to look at the data and give insights that might be valuable, and to make graphs. Everything done with ChatGPT-5.0 is double checked with standard statistic software (Python and Excel).

\subsection*{Transcript}
The transcription produced by the system went through three processing steps. First, Whisper converted the speech to text. Then, punctuation restoration was performed by FPML \cite{guhrEtAl}, and finally, diarization was carried out by pyannote \cite{Bredin23,Plaquet23}. Only the final result of this pipeline was saved and analysed in this section. The output of the transcription was a JSON object with four fields: \textit{start}, \textit{end}, \textit{speaker}, and \textit{text}. \textit{Start} marks the time in the audio recording when a speaker begins speaking, and \textit{end} marks when it finishes. \textit{Speaker} identifies the person speaking, and \textit{text} contains the spoken words.

When analysing this data, one issue becomes apparent immediately: the entries are unusually short for full sentences, and the same speaker frequently appears in consecutive entries that should have been merged into a single entry. To illustrate this problem, three plots were generated from the raw dataset. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:entry_duration_raw_merged}, the majority of entries are shorter than 2 seconds. Figure~\ref{fig:wordcount_raw_merged} shows that most entries contain only one to three words. In addition to short sentences, the text contains artifacts such as excess periods at the end of an entry and stray periods inserted within words. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:normalized_artifacts}, this is quite common, but it also varies substantially between sessions. To strengthen this observation, Figure~\ref{fig:raw_vs_artifacts} shows no correlation between the number of entries and the number of artifacts. These observations indicate clear diarization errors: the system failed to capture complete sentences as coherent entries. However, since many consecutive entries are assigned to the same speaker, merging them produces a much more accurate representation of the dialogue.

The merged dataset tells a very different story, as shown in Figures~\ref{fig:raw_vs_merged}, \ref{fig:entry_duration_raw_merged}, and \ref{fig:wordcount_raw_merged}. We see a significant reduction in the number of entries, indicating that much of the raw data resulted from over-segmentation. The number of short entries (1–2 seconds) is noticeably reduced—though they still dominate—while longer entries become more common, and their distribution better reflects the structure of spoken sentences. This highlights how fragmented the raw transcription was and demonstrates the necessity of merging consecutive same-speaker entries to produce usable data.

Considering the possibility that some entries might not be strictly consecutive in the JSON file but still represent a single sentence separated by a short pause or another speaker, an additional test merged entries from the same speaker if they were separated by a small time gap. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:entry_counts_merge_strategies}, applying thresholds of 2 seconds and 5 seconds produced substantial changes in the number of entries and brought the session counts much closer together. Furthermore, Figure~\ref{fig:normalized_entries_merge_strategies} shows that the number of entries per minute becomes very similar between sessions, suggesting a better-structured dataset. As a result, the merged versions are likely to provide higher-quality input for subsequent processing and yield more coherent results when generating the comic book.

Lastly, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:entry_duration_loose} and Figure~\ref{fig:wordcount_loose}, the distribution of entry durations becomes more spread out after time-based merging. While most entries remain only a few seconds long, the presence of more medium- and longer-length entries suggests the dataset now reflects sentence-level speech more accurately.

There are, of course, other ways to interpret the data (e.g., examining speaker activity, turn-taking, or thematic content). However, these approaches focus more on analysing the session itself than on improving data usability. Because the purpose of this thesis is to create a comic book rather than to analyse play sessions, alternative forms of analysis were deliberately left out in favour of methods that directly enhance comic-book generation.

\subsection*{Script generation}
The second step in the pipeline is the creation of the comic book script. This step outputs a JSON file with 8 different keys: \texttt{Setting}, \texttt{Characters}, \texttt{Objects}, \texttt{Actions}, \texttt{Mood}, \texttt{Camera\_view}, \texttt{Lighting}, and \texttt{Text}. These 8 keys are created by Llama~3.1-8B by converting the output of the transcription. The goal is to create enough relevant data which Llama can afterwards use to generate a prompt for the image generation model.

When analysing this data across all four sessions there are a couple of aspects where things are going wrong, but also some things that went well. First, the data structure is always the same, and that is very good as it is the only way for it to be useful in the next step of the pipeline. This consistency is a result of an output check after Llama produces the JSON. However, the \texttt{text} field is not always filled, which is a problem for the comic book as it will result in an empty speech bubble. Having a filled \texttt{text} key is something that should be added to the check function. Next, the character names are very inconsistent and have strange structures: \textit{Go.nor.r.hea} vs. \textit{Gonorrhea} vs. \textit{Gonorrohea} (players tend to give their characters unusual names). But also names like \textit{D.M.}, \textit{Gonorrhea's defeated body}, and \textit{Gonorrhea (in dragon form)} occur occasionally. If you want to create consistency within a product this needs to be cleaned up. It is most likely a result of the transcription process or Llama being confused by strange words that do not link properly to normal language usage.  

There is also quite a bit of repetition within the settings, camera, and mood sections. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:camera-distribution} we can see that most camera views generated by the system are \textit{Medium shot}. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:mood-distribution} we can see that most moods are \textit{Tense/Uneasy}, with only a handful of other moods appearing consistently. The ``Other'' category is actually a diverse collection of moods that did not fit into the chosen bins. However, this aligns with the sessions the players played: they were run in a very tense and fast-paced style where the Dungeon Master pushed the players into quick and dramatic encounters. The same holds true if we look at the grouped settings in Figure~\ref{fig:setting-distribution}, where we can clearly see that they are in line with what the sessions were about. The ``Other'' category here primarily consists of mistakes where the system inserted locations that were never part of the adventure, such as \textit{hotel lobby}, \textit{tropical beach}, or \textit{railway corridor}. While not all entries in this category are errors, many are.  

The \texttt{objects} key is rather inconsistent. Sometimes it gives nice meaningful input like \textit{Chalice of Eternal Life} or \textit{Guard key}. However, it also includes things that are not objects at all: people, descriptions of people, or animals. Even when it does describe the same thing across panels, it does so inconsistently, for example, first it is \textit{cat in the distance}, then \textit{mysterious cat}, and then \textit{a small, unimpressed cat}. There is no clear link between these descriptions other than that they all refer to a cat.  

Another aspect that should not be part of the data at this point is gameplay leakage: words like \textit{AC}, \textit{damage}, \textit{roll}, or \textit{check} in the context of rules discussions. A script was run to count the number of such terms, using the set of keywords \footnote{The following mechanics-related keywords were used to detect gameplay leakage:  roll, rolled, rolling, dice, die, d20, d12, d10, d8, d6, d4, nat 20, nat 1, natural 20, natural 1, check, skill, saving throw, save, acrobatics, animal handling, arcana, athletics, deception, history, insight, intimidation, investigation, medicine, nature, perception, performance, persuasion, religion, sleight of hand, stealth, survival, initiative, AC (armor class), hit points (HP), damage (dmg), critical (crit), advantage, disadvantage, rule, rules.}. As shown in Table~\ref{tab:leakage-summary} and Table~\ref{tab:leakage-all-sessions}, the percentage of entries with gameplay leakage is small but non-negligible as if any of these appear in the comic book it will break immersion. The system should be improved to avoid this kind of leakage, either by filtering out such entries or by instructing Llama to ignore them.

\subsection*{Prompt generation}
The third step in the pipeline is prompt generation. In this step, the structured script data (setting, characters, objects, action, mood, camera view, lighting, and text) is converted into a descriptive natural-language prompt suitable for the image generation model (SDXL 1.0), or at least that is the goal. The purpose of this process is to bridge the gap between structured data and natural language generation, to let the LLM be a creative member of the process and take its place as the creator of the comic book. This step of generating the prompt is both the most creative and also the most error-prone step. The JSON keys provide structured anchors, while the prompt reformulates them into language that SDXL can interpret.

When analysing the generated prompts across all four sessions, they initially seem quite strong. The prompts are very descriptive, they consistently include character names, lighting, shadows, objects, scene setting, location, mood, specific things to showcase, emotions, and more. Each prompt is also very consistent in terms of what it produces and how it is structured: the same keys appear each time and in roughly the same order. On their own the prompts create vivid descriptions of the situation. However, there are also some very obvious artifacts, such as the inclusion of "Here is the generated SDXL prompt:" or "/n/n" at the start of prompts, along with other task-descriptive text that should never be part of the final input. Many prompts are also longer than the supposed 75-token limit, which causes issues for SDXL as it cuts off the end of the prompt. There is no concrete data on this, as no tokenizer for SDXL is openly available, but the model frequently returned errors stating that prompts were too long. These cut-offs happened often, although no counter was in place to track how many times. Errors made during transcription are also carried forward into the prompts, as the same artifacts reappear in the generated text (extra dots, inconsistent names, and so on). Besides the quality and errors of each individual prompt, there are also issues across prompts and some overarching structural problems.

When looking at individual prompts they seem relatively usable because of the consistencies: locations, characters, and objects are almost always included. However, when attempting to understand the story from prompt to prompt it quickly becomes clear that there is no real throughline. Almost all prompts suffer from non-sequitur transitions \cite{mccloud1993understanding}. There is no flow, no continuity, no linking of events. Each prompt functions as an isolated moment in time, without context to what came before or after.

Another recurring issue is that while each prompt references a lot of items, objects, locations, and characters, this information is lost in the image generation step. SDXL has no way of knowing who Arthur is, what the Chalice of Eternal Life looks like, or how Dar.yl is supposed to appear. The model simply has to guess based on its training data. This leads to strange or inconsistent images, where characters change appearance between panels or important objects are rendered incorrectly or not at all (See next section for details). In other words, the prompts are rich in detail, but this detail cannot be grounded in any shared visual database, which creates a significant gap between text and image in the pipeline.

Overall, prompt generation illustrates an issue between fidelity and creativity. On the one hand, the prompts succeed in faithfully translating structured data into richly descriptive text. On the other hand, they also reveal the instability of relying on an LLM to act as a creator. artifacts, hallucinations, and missing continuity highlight how difficult this step is, and how much of the story is reshaped by the model itself. In this sense, prompt generation is both the strongest and weakest part of the pipeline: it produces the most vivid material, but it also introduces the greatest risk of distortion in the retelling.

\subsection*{Image generation}
The final step of generating the images for the comic book is ultimately the key part and goal of the entire pipeline. Because of this, it depends on all previous steps, and any problems in those steps fully echo into the generated image. This stage showcases both the limits of the system and the potential and possibilities it offers. This section analyses the issues of individual images, but also of the comic book as a whole and how the images are impacted by earlier steps.\footnote{See all comic books: https://github.com/BubbleCoding/CandlekeepComicbooks}.

On the level of single panels, the images often suffer from common generative artifacts such as fused objects, incorrect anatomy, or floating elements (see Figure~\ref{fig:example_panels_grid_f}). In addition, comic-specific conventions such as Manpu or speech bubbles are almost entirely absent, appearing only rarely and seemingly by accident (see Figure~\ref{fig:example_panels_grid_i}). When combining text and image, it is often difficult to find a meaningful link between the two. For example, in Figure~\ref{fig:example_panels_grid_c}, the caption refers to Tevin trying to grab a cat, but the corresponding image depicts only cats. This misalignment can be traced back to errors in script generation and transcription. In many cases, the text is not dialogue at all, but rather descriptive or explanatory narration, which reads as strange in a comic book context. One area in which the system consistently succeeded, however, was in producing exposition panels (Figure~\ref{fig:example_panels_grid_a}), which provide atmospheric establishing shots.

You might assume that some of these images are a result of the model hallucinating content, but this is not the case. If we look at the prompt for Figure~\ref{fig:example_panels_grid_j}: \textit{"Inside a swirling portal of rainbow colors, Syphilis and Gonorrhea walk hand-in-hand through shimmering, kaleidoscopic lights as they step into the unknown. Blinking colors dance around them from an aerial view."} we can see that the image corresponds closely to the prompt. Furthermore, when examining the other keys generated during script creation, this alignment is confirmed. Analysis of additional examples shows the same pattern: the image generation step is generally doing what it is supposed to do, namely generating an image faithful to the prompt. The problem is that the prompt is not always correct or useful, and that SDXL has no way of knowing what a specific character or object should look like. Addressing this issue requires improvements to the prompt generation step, and potentially the use of an image generation model that can incorporate more structured information about characters and objects.

Many of the creatures that appear in the story are also wrongly represented in the generated comic book. For example, the two-headed ogre (Figure~\ref{fig:example_panels_grid_o}), the owlbear (Figure~\ref{fig:example_panels_grid_n}), and the vampire (Figure~\ref{fig:example_panels_grid_k}) are all depicted inaccurately. The model does not possess knowledge of these fantasy creatures beyond general cultural approximations, and therefore produces images that are best understood as guesses rather than faithful representations. This highlights a key limitation of prompt-based generation: without explicit visual grounding or additional training data, the system cannot reliably render domain-specific entities that fall outside of its training set.

When looking at the generated images as a comic book, a number of broader aspects stand out. First, the art style of the images is both consistent and inconsistent. Because a LoRA was used to encourage a uniform style, there is indeed some degree of visual coherence. However, the themes across images vary wildly: shifting from greyscale portraits of serious-looking characters, to grim pirate taverns, to rainbow-coloured psychedelic scenes, to futuristic soldiers, and even to World War II imagery and superheroes. These thematic inconsistencies disrupt the reading experience and highlight the limits of relying on prompt-based generation for sequential art. Secondly, there is no visual consistency between panels: characters are almost always portrayed differently, locations change drastically even in back-to-back panels, and similar continuity issues arise. This again results from the absence of a through line in the prompts, as well as the lack of shared context between individual image generations. Lastly, one strength that is present in the comic book is the overarching through line of the entire story. Although the comic book fails as a readable artifact, it does still represent the story the players experienced. The key events are there, the characters are there, and the main plot points are there. It is simply not a good retelling of the story. This tension between representation and retelling highlights the broader challenge: while the pipeline succeeds in capturing the story's structure, it struggles to convey it coherently in the medium of comics.

In summary, the image generation stage demonstrates both the strengths and weaknesses of the overall pipeline. On the one hand, the model reliably follows the prompts it is given and produces visually striking exposition panels. On the other hand, errors from earlier steps are magnified here, leading to misaligned text–image pairs, missing comic-specific conventions, inaccurate depictions of domain-specific creatures, and thematic inconsistency across the comic book. These findings suggest that the primary challenges do not lie in the generative model itself, but in the quality and specificity of the information it receives. Improving prompt generation, integrating stronger grounding for characters and creatures, and introducing comic-aware conventions are therefore essential directions for enhancing the fidelity and coherence of future retellings.

\subsection*{Conclusion}
Across all stages of the pipeline, the analysis reveals several concrete design requirements for improving the system. At the transcription level, diarization must be refined to avoid over-segmentation and ensure sentence-level coherence. Script generation requires stricter output validation: all fields, especially \texttt{text}, should be populated, character names must be normalised, and inconsistent or irrelevant data should be filtered. Prompt generation needs mechanisms for maintaining narrative continuity between panels, reducing artifacts, and creating consistency with characters and objects in their appearance. Finally, the image generation stage highlights the need for stronger stylistic consistency, clearer differentiation of characters and locations, support for comic-specific conventions such as manpu, and improved visual quality for fantasy-specific settings. Together, these requirements provide a clear roadmap for enhancing the pipeline's ability to produce coherent, faithful, and readable retellings. Despite its current limitations, the pipeline shows clear potential as a tool for transforming recorded play sessions into a new expressive medium. With targeted refinements guided by the identified design requirements, such a system could evolve from an experimental prototype into a powerful method for retelling TTRPG experiences.

\section{Chapter Conclusion}
The analyses presented in this chapter show that the current prototype succeeds in capturing important aspects of retelling tabletop role playing game sessions while also revealing significant limitations in how those sessions are retold as comics. The self-observation of the pipeline highlights how small errors at each stage, from broken transcription and inconsistent script data generation to messy prompts and lack of image generation control, accumulate and combine into a inconsistent and chaotic comic book. The questionnaire responses reflect and reinforce these findings from the user perspective: participants recognised the atmosphere and key events of their sessions and felt a sense of authorship in the resulting comic, yet they also reported issues with chronology, visual and narrative consistency, dialogue attribution, and overall readability. Both forms of analysis point to clear design requirements for future development, including improved transcription merging, stricter data validation, mechanisms for narrative continuity, relatable visual references for characters and objects, and greater stylistic consistency across panels. Most importantly the results show that the core concept of retelling TTRPG sessions as comics through AI is both technically feasible and meaningful to players. Addressing the identified shortcomings will be essential for transforming the system from a prototype into a robust tool capable of producing coherent, engaging, and ethically acceptable retellings.
