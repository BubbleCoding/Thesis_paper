\section{Retelling games}
This section explores the theoretical background of retelling games. It tries to define what retelling is, how narrative, media studies, and game studies can help us understand it, and why retellings are meaningful in both creative and analytical contexts.

\subsection*{Retelling a definition}
Game retelling is the act of reconstructing a past play experience into a new form, whether through storytelling, video, illustration, or another medium. It is a way of making sense of what happened during play, translating fleeting moments into shareable narratives. In analogue games like TTRPGs, where much of the story unfolds through improvisation and player interaction, retelling can serve many purposes. It preserves memories, communicates the experience to others, and allows for reflection on the themes, decisions, and dynamics that emerged. Whether casually recounted around a table, written into detailed session reports, or adapted into comics and podcasts, game retellings are always interpretive. They emphasize certain moments over others, shift the tone or point of view, and reshape nonlinear, messy play into something structured and communicable.

Retelling is not unique to games. It is a deeply human practice, as old as humanity itself. Across history, people have retold experiences and stories to remember, explain, warn, reinterpret, or entertain. Whether through oral traditions, books, historical records, films, podcasts, or personal anecdotes, the act of retelling transforms raw experience into structured narrative \cite{storr2019science}. Game retelling continues this tradition: it is a way of making meaning out of play, translating fleeting moments into lasting stories that can be shared, reshaped, and remembered.

Retelling a game is not simply an act of preservation; it is an act of authorship. The moment one begins to retell, decisions must be made: what events matter, which moments carry meaning, and how they should be presented. These choices reshape the original experience, transforming play into narrative. In this sense, retelling becomes a creative process of selection, remixing, and reinterpretation. It is a form of participatory media production that aligns with what Jenkins calls convergence culture: a context in which stories circulate across platforms and audiences actively contribute to their expansion and transformation \cite{jenkins2008convergence}.

Entertaining a retelling of a game is different than retelling a game. The one entertaining it is a passive observer, a voyeur of the story, while the reteller is still an active participant, picking one branch out of the branching narrative to retell in whatever way they see fit. The reteller keeps a level of agency which they can use to see the result of their decisions and choices.

\subsection*{Theoretical Foundation}
The retelling of a game is a beautiful example of the difference between cybertexts and traditional literature \cite{aarseth1995cybertext}. In cybertexts, the reader is not just a reader, but a player. They are not just consuming a linear narrative, but they are shaping and discovering an interactive narrative through their effort and interaction with the text. While a retelling of a game is an experience where you as the reader are a voyeur, a passive observer of a story once created by the players. A retelling is not a pure reconstruction of the original play experience, but rather a paratext in the form of a new interpretation of it \cite{gray2010show}. The reteller selects, emphasizes, and sometimes even alters events to create a coherent narrative that captures the essence of the original play while making it accessible to an audience that was not present during the game.

In a retelling of a game, much of the original experience is inevitably lost. The original play experience is not just a story, but a complex interplay of rules, player agency, open-endedness, speculation, creativity, and player interactions. We are going from an interactive experience to an immersive one \cite{ryan2015narrative}, sacrificing agency in favour of structured narrative delivery. The act of retelling removes most of the branching narrative which is essential to gameplay and converts it into a linear or semi-linear retelling. We lose a level of narrative simulation which is entrusted to us while using more ergodic texts \cite{aarseth1995cybertext}. 

A retelling of games is often a half-real experience that leans toward fictional narrative \cite{juul2011half}. The reteller aims to create a coherent story that can be understood as a standalone artifact, without requiring the audience to grasp the game's underlying rules. Yet those rules are precisely what shaped the original story. The fact that a player failed disastrously due to a bad dice roll, or triumphed in a hopeless situation through sheer luck, is not just incidental, it is central to the experience. Weaving rule-based elements into the fictional retelling is essential for capturing the full texture of the original play experience.

Recapping is also a form of retelling. Many ongoing TV shows, series, anime, comic books, etc. tend to start each episode with a retelling of the previous episodes, focusing only on the important aspects and leaving out a lot of the scenes. This is done in an attempt to reengage the watcher/reader and to remind them of the flow of the story so that they understand the story better. The same counts for TTRPG groups who are part of a larger story that lasts numerous sessions separated by time. This is done in an attempt to restructure the story and make it clear to all the players to understand where they are in the story. Every time a story is retold it can ascribe new symbolic value through the way it is retold, as the paratext is variable between retellings \cite{gray2010show}. 

Eladhari introduces the concept of game retelling as the fourth layer of narrative \cite{eladhari2018re}. According to Eladhari, this layer exists beyond the embedded, emergent, and interpreted narratives that typically characterize game storytelling. The fourth layer is concerned with post-play reflection and reinterpretation. It allows players, designers, or critics to re-engage with a game experience after the fact, often through a different medium \cite{eladhari2018re}. In this view, retelling is not just about conveying what happened in the game, it is also about creating space for analysis, critique, or emotional processing.

Sych expands on this concept by arguing that critical game retellings can serve as a bridge between personal experience and broader thematic exploration. By combining Eladhari's fourth layer with the idea of “breaking the fourth wall” Sych shows how retellings can expose the mechanics, social dynamics, and ethical tensions of gameplay itself \cite{sych2020fourth}. This is particularly relevant in TTRPGs, where storytelling and rules are deeply intertwined, and where players often make decisions that reflect not just in-game motivations, but also real-world values and group norms.

Moreover, game retelling in this form invites consideration of what is lost and what is gained in translation. Elements like humor, tension, or spontaneity may be flattened or reshaped, while the visual format offers new tools, framing, symbolism, and visual rhythm to convey meaning. As Eladhari notes, retelling is not just representational; it is generative. It produces new readings, new interpretations, and sometimes even new stories.

Finally, comic-based retelling also offers a lens into how different types of player interaction, such as roleplay, exposition, and rule engagement, manifest in a visual medium. The project thus becomes a way to explore not only how games are retold, but also how different aspects of a game are retold by artificial intelligence.

\subsection{Examples of TTRPG retellings}
Many people post their TTRPG sessions on the internet as podcasts and/or as video content. These could be seen as retellings as they are most of the time modified versions of the original, however there are some of these shows that go above and beyond and convert their original recording into a true retelling. In this section we look at some of these examples to ground the concept of retelling TTRPGs as something that people want, but also to learn from the retellings and as a source of inspiration.

Critical Role, one of the largest TTRPG podcast shows out there \cite{criticalrole_liveplay}, converted their adventures into \textit{The Legends of Vox Machina}\cite{criticalrole_legends_vox_machina}, a polished animated retelling of their podcast episodes. It is heavily scripted as it truly turns a chaotic TTRPG session into a smooth TV experience. They also created two comic books from their work: \textit{Critical Role: Vox Machina Origins}\cite{criticalrole_vox_machina_origins} and \textit{Critical Role: The Mighty Nein Origins}\cite{criticalrole_mighty_nein_origins}. These are, however, primarily focused on new information and function as a prequel.

The Adventure Zone is a TTRPG podcast \cite{adventurezone_podcast} that retold their original work as a comic book series \cite{AdventureZone2018}. The comics are a beautiful combination of a standalone series, but keep many meta elements of \textit{Dungeons and Dragons} inside of it. The Dungeon Master sometimes makes a cameo in a separate panel where they interact with the world or comment on the world, giving the person that would not have a prominent and obvious role a place to shine. Through this it also stays close to the source material and does not try to change the original story too much. The comic book series is a retelling of the podcast, but it is also a retelling of the game that was played, having both narrative and game mechanics retold.

\section{Comics as a Medium for Retelling}
Creating comic books is an art form on its own, and to understand how to generate a comic book with artificial intelligence, we need to understand how comic books function as an artifact: how they are made, how they are structured, how they are read, and what comic books are. This section will look at comic book studies to create a better understanding of comic books as an artifact.

\subsection*{Defining Comics}
Just like how Scott McCloud starts his book \textit{Understanding Comics} we should also ask ourselves: What even is a comic book? Luckily we can just take the definition that McCloud gives us: "A comic book is a juxtaposed pictorial and other images in deliberate sequence, intended to convey information and/or to produce an aesthetic response in the viewer." \cite{mccloud1993understanding}. This definition is very complex and for the purpose of this thesis we can simplify it to sequential art \cite{eisner2008comics}. When you take a comic book panel and look at it outside of the context of other panels it is just an image. But when you place them side by side in a sequential order the pictures become a comic book \ref{fig:SequentialArt}. Looking at the image we can see that an image of a man holding its hat is just a man holding a hat. But when you place two images of a man holding his hat side by side we can see that it creates a motion of tipping his hat. The panels create an effect of change, an effect of time passing. The space between the panels, knows as the "gutter", create a concept of time in stationary images. Placing images in a sequential manner is the essence of comic books.

\subsection*{Comic Book Formats and Reading Conventions}

Comic books exist in a wide variety of formats and traditions, each with its own structural and cultural conventions. When people think of comic books, they often picture brightly coloured superhero stories or comedic adventures they read as children. While these are certainly part of the medium's history, they represent only one branch of a much broader spectrum of comic book formats.

One of the most widely known formats is Japanese \textit{manga}. Manga is typically published in black and white, uses a right-to-left reading order, and often follows a clear panel structure with high variation in panel size and pacing. Manga tends to place a strong emphasis on emotion, movement, and stylized facial expressions, and often incorporates visual manpu symbols to represent feelings or actions.

American comics, on the other hand, are usually published in full color and follow a left-to-right reading order. They tend to use grid-based layouts, with clearly delineated panels and speech bubbles. While the most globally recognized genres in American comics are superhero stories, the format is used for everything from noir and horror to memoir and historical fiction.

Korean \textit{manhwa}, and especially digital webtoons, represent another major tradition. These are primarily designed for vertical scrolling on digital devices, often in full color. Unlike page-based comics, webtoons use elongated vertical panels and spacing to control pacing and emotional beats. This format has become increasingly popular globally, thanks to the easy accessibility of webcomics on the internet.

Beyond these formats, there are countless experimental and hybrid approaches. European \textit{bande dessinée} , Chinese \textit{lianhuanhua}, and graphic novels each bring their own conventions. Some comics, such as Frank Miller's Sin City, use high-contrast black-and-white art to create a noir aesthetic \cite{miller1991sincity}. Others, like those illustrated by David Finch \cite{finch2024portfolio}, are known for hyper-detailed linework and realism. Comics are an art form and thus infinite ways of representing them exist.

These different comic book formats are not just stylistic choices, they influence how stories are told, how information is revealed, and how readers interpret visual cues. For any system that seeks to generate comics, understanding these formats is crucial. The chosen structure will affect panel composition, pacing, and even the kind of narrative that can be effectively conveyed.

\subsection*{Comic Book Panels}
One of the core aspects of comics is the panel structure chosen by the artist. Some comics even break conventional panel structure and use whole pages as panels, place panels in panels, have panels that are not rectangular, but irregular shapes such as curved, circular, or angled layouts. Some do not even use panels at all but make use of emptiness or use the same character on a long panel with multiple instances of the same character creating a concept of time as you read left to right. Comic storytelling embraces a wide variety of panelling styles and they shape the comic book.

In between each panel there is a space of nothingness known as the gutter. This is the place where two panels transition from two separate images to one idea \cite{mccloud1993understanding}. Thought just an empty white space on a page we as humans fill in this empty space with what would/could happen to go from the one to the next panel. The reader becomes an active participant in the story they fill in the gaps. This location of transition has been classified by McCloud into 6 different forms of transition \ref{fig:panelTransitions}. This however is not a hard science, but it gives us a better understanding of comics and it can be useful when analysing the narrative structure of the generated comic.

A Moment-to-Moment transition is a small change. On the first panel the person has their eyes open and on the next their eyes are closed. On the first panel a hand is on a table and on the next panel the hand is 2 inches to the left on the table.

An Action-to-Action transition is a more impactful and instant change. In the first panel a person stands ready to hit a baseball with his bat and in the second panel they hit the ball. 

A Subject-To-Subject transition is a change of subject while staying within the scene. Switching a panel from one person to another person who are talking with each other. Switching between someone trying to defuse a bomb and the timer on the bomb. These type of panels require the reader to exert more effort to understand what is happening.

A Scene-to-Scene transition is a change of scene which transitions across significant time and space. The first panel is a kid running out of the front door of his house and the next panel being the kid entering the school front door. Two panels showing two people thinking about each other while both siting on their own bed in their own house. These type of panels require even more effort from the reader.

An Aspect-to-Aspect transition is different as it bypasses time and focuses on different aspects of a place, a mood or an idea. Showing a number of panels which together create the scene. Create understanding of someone enjoying their holiday by showing them sunbath, followed by a panel of the ocean followed by a panel of the sky with the sun. These type of transitions anchor the reader into the world.

Lastly Non-Sequitur transitions are a transition that offers no logical relationship at all. Switching from a boy making his math exam to a painting of a chicken to the sun to a white wall. There is no underlying connection between these panels.

Besides the transitions of panels there is also an importance on how many transitions and panel one uses to convey an idea. If a comic uses more panels to convey an idea the scene will most likely become more vivid for the reader as there is a lot more context. If there are too little panels there might even be too little context for the reader to understand what is happening. More panels could thus be valuable if you try to visualize a very intense and important moment.

\subsection*{Text, iconography and speech bubbles}

Text in comic books is one of the primary ways to convey information to the reader. It is used to convey dialogue, thoughts, sound effects and narration. The way text is presented can greatly affect the reading experience. Just like while writing this thesis I have to use \textit{italics} to emphasize a word, use \textbf{bold} almost exclusively for headings, and I could use \underline{underlines} but that is no where to be found in this text. Comics also make use of these different text styles to convey different meanings. Use bold to put emphasize on something, use italics to indicate something is fast or to indicate a thought, and use underlines to indicate something is important and there are way more forms of typographic emphasis than these three and also way more ways they are being used. 

Iconography is essential to many comic books to communicate those things that can not be easily spoken or written down. In the world of manga we call this unspoken communication \textit{Manpu} \ref{fig:Iconography}. To quote Matt Alt \textit{ Manpu "speak" what can't be easily spoken (or written) in words} \cite{GigaTown}. "ZZZ" to indicate someone is snoring or sleeping, a circle with starts and birds above a characters head indicate a state of unconsciousness by an impact of sorts, pages being torn of a calendar indicate time passing and one of my favourite making an L with your thumb and index finger and placing it under you chin to indicate deep thought (or the one I do not like where it represent people acting cool).

The frequency, amount, size, typographic emphasis all have different effect on this use case of words creating different meanings with different or the same words. These sounds are not just there to indicate what is happening, but also to create an atmosphere and to make the reader feel like they are part of the story.

\section{AI Ethics in Creative Applications} \label{AIethics}
This section discusses the ethical consideration when creating an AI system based on the ethical guidelines for trustworthy AI given by the European union \cite{europeancommission2019ethics}. This document consist of 4 Ethical principles which should be followed and 7 key requirements which any AI system should follow. The goal of this section is to understand what aspects to keep in mind during development, but also to have a ruler to measure the final artifact against to see if the resulting artifact is in line with European guidelines and if not why that is the case.

\subsection{4 Ethical Principles  - Introduction}
The ethics guidelines for trustworthy AI gives us 4 ethical principles which should be followed to create an ethical and just AI system. These AI ethics are grounded in the fundamental rights within the EU treaties, the EU Charter and the international human rights law.  Furthermore these guidelines are overarching guidelines based partly on a more difference set of principles proposed by the European Group on Ethics in Science and New Technologies\cite{floridi2018ai4people}. These guidelines are however not without their issues and might have tension between them where the designer will have to make an acceptable ethical trade-off for their system, if possible. Some of the aspects of the ethical principles have been left out as those are way out of scope for this project and academic research in general.

\subsubsection*{The principle of respect for human autonomy}
This principle discusses the idea that human beings need to have freedom and autonomy and that an AI system should not interfere with these rights. The goal of AI systems should be to augment, complement and empower human beings in aspects of life. And AI systems should be there to support humans to create meaningful work which can contribute to the world. Because of this the design of AI systems should be done from human-centric design principles, resulting in humans to still take the meaningful and potential impactful opportunities up to humans. Humans should always have oversight over AI work processes. Lastly any human being using an AI system should have full and effective self-determination over themselves.


\subsubsection*{The principle of prevention of harm}
This principle is in short the first law of Robotics from Asimov: A robot may not injure a human being or allow a human to come to harm through inaction. In further detail the principle says that an AI system should not harm or otherwise adversely affect human beings. This is not only about physical safety, but also about human dignity and mental health. To ensure this a system should be technically robust and not open to malicious use. The extent of prevention of harm does not stop with humans, as it should consider the natural environment and all living beings. Lastly vulnerable persons and asymmetries of power or information should receive particular attention when designing AI systems.

\subsubsection*{The principle of fairness}
This principle discusses the idea that development, deployment and use of AI systems should be fair and if possible should create more fairness within the world. The prevention of bias, discrimination and stigmatization should always be a core part of any AI system. Fairness also extends to equal opportunity in terms of access to AI technology, educations, goods and services and people working in the sphere should foster this. Most importantly is that AI systems should never deceive or unjustifiably impair people of their freedom of choice. Because of this the decision-making processes should be identifiable and explainable by humans.

\subsubsection*{The principle of explicability}
This principle discusses the explicability of AI systems and their components as this is crucial for building and maintaining trust is AI systems. In essence this means that a system should be transparent, openly communicated about its capabilities and purpose, the output of the system should be explainable.  Sometimes there are AI systems which can not be explained and those require special attention. In these cases special attention should be paid to explicability and other ways of explaining the system should be considered, such as traceability, auditability and transparent communication on system capabilities. Explicability is however in a weird position as it is not very concrete and can differ widely in how much is required depending on the AI system and its context.

\subsection{Realizing Trustworthy AI 7 requirements}
This section discusses the 7 key requirements which AI systems to follow to be become trustworthy AI systems. These requirements are build upon the 4 ethical guidelines. Furthermore they are accompanied by technical and non-technical methods to achieve this goal.
\subsubsection*{Human agency and oversight}
In essence all AI systems should support human autonomy and decision-making for this the first step is that the system protects the fundamental rights of people. In cases where this is a risk, a fundamental rights impact assessment should be done. Furthermore users of the AI system should be able to supply feedback in case the fundament rights might be infringed. The second step is to give users the knowledge and tools to understand and interact with the AI system in such a way that they can assess the system and challenge the system. Ai systems should further the capability of humans and support them achieve the goals they want to achieve. The focus should be on improving human autonomy and thus it should avoid (incidental) manipulation, deception, herding and conditioning. Lastly to achieve human autonomy, the system should have enough oversight capabilities so that humans can override any decisions made by the system.
\subsubsection*{Technical robustness and safety}
Trustworthy AI systems should have a certain degree of technical robustness. This requires that during the development of an AI system to extra care is taken around risk mitigation and prevention to such an extent that the system reliably behave in the way it is designed to behave. It should also have a robust security system in place to protect itself and its data against outside threats. AI systems should have fall back options so that if the system fails to function there are other systems or humans that can be used to take its role. The system should also have a high level of accuracy in terms of what it outputs and in cases where occasional inaccurate predictions can not be prevented it should be made clear that this is the case and how likely this is to happen. Lastly AI systems should have a high level of reproducibility as well as reliability. 
\subsubsection*{Privacy and data governance}
As with all IT systems that use data it is of vital importance that privacy of data is guaranteed and in accordance with the GDPR. Thus data needs to be handled with care and not used in any unlawful manner or used in an unfairly discriminating manner. Data that is used to train AI should also be addressed before it is used as the integrity of the data needs to be ensured. 
\subsubsection*{Transparency}
Within the domain of transparency there are 3 topics, traceability, explainability and communication. Traceability means that the data that is used to train models should be documented to the best possible standards and the the way the AI system makes decisions is documented. This all to increase transparency, which allows developers to see why a system could create wrong outputs. Explainability entails to being able to explain why an AI system functions in the way it does and why it is used in certain contexts. Lastly communication entails that the user of an AI system is aware that it is using an AI system and not for example talking with an AI chatbot instead of a human customer support agent. Furthermore the end user should understand what the capabilities and limitations are of a AI system.
\subsubsection*{Diversity, non-discrimination and fairness}
This requirement focuses on the inclusion and diversity throughout AI system's their life cycles, from design to development to operation. The diversification of AI systems should be supported by the unbiased datasets and diverse development teams with different backgrounds, cultures and disciplines. Identifiable bias in datasets should be removed during the data collection and a diverse development team should be garnered during the hiring process. Furthermore AI systems should be accessible to all people and should not behold a one-size-fits-all approach, but instead understand that there are different people with different needs and means. Lastly it is important to discuss the AI system with stakeholders to gather feedback on the system so that it can become a more fair system.
\subsubsection*{Environmental and societal well-being}
AI systems should think about the impact they can have on the broader society, other sentient beings and the environment. The entire life cycle of an AI system should be considered in term of energy consumption and resource usage with the goal to have a less bad effect on the environment. The social impact as a result of certain AI systems should also be be considered as certain systems can have impact on how people behave and think. Lastly AI systems should not threat upon the democratic process of society or other societal perspectives.
\subsubsection*{Accountability}
The accountability requirement ties all the requirements together by placing accountability on the creators of AI systems and their outcomes. To comply with this an AI system, its data and the design process should have a high enough level of auditability so that the trustworthiness of the system could be tested. In case the trustworthiness is in question there should be mechanisms that allow people to report on these issue in a safe manner with the overarching goal to minimize negative impact from AI systems.

When working with the requirements stated above tensions between them is bound to appear and in such a situation trade-offs need to be made. These trade-offs should be considered carefully in a rational and methodological manner. This way if any problems arise it can be documenter ad used to better the system in the future. This way an AI system can garner most trust from its users and become a better system in the end.

\section{Best practices for prompt engineering}
Prompting any langue model is a kin to instructing a human, however the machine will take everything you say without context and thus prompting is a skill on its own. We can it prompt engineering for a reason after all. This section goes over important aspects that you should keep in mind while prompting based on articles of creators of different AI models. Specifically meta their guide to Llama \cite{MetaPrompting} and OpenAI's guide to their models \cite{OpenAIBestPractices}. Furthermore some guide that are floating around the internet for Stable Diffusion are discussed \cite{SDPrompting,SDModels}. Lastly a one article is used to string everything together \cite{ekin2023prompt}. 

\subsection{What is prompt engineering?}
Prompt engineering is the act of writing, refining, designing, and optimizing an instruction (the prompt) for a large language model. Resulting in a effective way of interacting with the model. The goal is to communicate the user's intent in such a way that both the machine and user have the same idea as to what needs to happen. This process is an essential step when working with LLM's if you want to obtain accurate, relevant and coherent responses. As LLM's are becoming better and more prevalent in our daily life understanding how to do prompt engineering will become a critical skill for anyone wanting to make full use of LLM's.

\subsection{Large language model prompting}
The prompting of large language models has become a whole discipline in the world and I assume it will become part of many people their daily life and in academia it will become part of courses and research. This section however will go into what the best practices are when instructing LLM's based on the instructions of the creators and articles on the web.
\subsubsection*{Be clear and concise}
A prompt should be straight to the point and easy to understand so that it can generate the output that you are looking for. It should provide just  information for the model to understand what to do and it shouldn't consist of too much information as the model might get confused from a larger context. Lastly it is important to avoid jargon and technical terms as the model might confuse those words with the wrong meaning.
\subsubsection*{Use specific examples}
When you are looking for specific output from the model it is important to provide and example of what the output should look like. Thus if you want a specific JSON format output you should provide that specific output once so that the model can work with the example and follow your instruction better.
\subsubsection*{Test and refine}
Just like engineering and writing, it is all about iteration. Testing the prompt you write and refining it over and over again will result in a better result in the end. This is also the best way to learn how to work with prompts and will make you a better prompt engineer in the long run. You should start with a zero-shot prompting approach, this is where give the model the bare minimum of instructions and see what the results are. If this is not enough you can move to few-shot  prompting which is where you provide a couple of examples of what you want. And if that is still not enough you can move to fine-tuning the model.
\subsubsection*{Role based prompts}
If you are looking for the model to respond in a certain way, you can instruct the model to embody a specific role. The role can be anything from a university professor or a student to a tour guide or a tech support. This way the model understand the context it is operating from better and it understand the perspective of the person interacting with the model better allowing for a more tailored response. 
\subsubsection*{Chain of Thought technique}
The chain of thought technique is a way to describe to the language model how it should think about and respond to the question it is being asked in a specific order. First do X, then do Y, then do Z, etc. This way the LLM knows exactly what to do and it can easily follow you instructions.
\subsubsection*{Reduce fluff and imprecise descriptions}
Do not leave your instruction ambiguous. If you tell the model to give a few sentences, it will not be very consistent and it might not be in line with what you thought. Say 3 to 5 sentences instead. Avoiding fluff also makes it so that your instructions are clearer to both yourself and the language model. Resulting in a better results.
\subsubsection*{Say what to do, not what not to do}
A large language model cuts your instructions up into pieces and then converts it to number making a guess for what to do respond with. Thus if you say something like: Do not ask for someone's age. It might lose the context of the not part and ask the age of someone. Because of this is it better to be precise about what the model should do and to avoid things that it shouldn't do.
\subsubsection*{Tone and style}
The tone and style you write your prompts in dictates the tone and style the model will respond in. If you ask very academic question in a very academic way it will respond in an academic manner. If you ask questions in a more free form and/or childish form it will respond in kind.
\subsubsection*{Leading words}
If you are using the model for very specific tasks such as code or prompt generation it is important to words that are linked to that. So if you want code for a specific project, mention the coding language you use and the way you want to run it. For example if making a web application with python, you should mention that you are using Python and which libraries you want to use such.
\subsubsection*{Prompt order}
The order you write your prompt in an important way to make sure that the model understand what you are asking of it. A prompt can have many parts such as: Task, example, role, goal, etc. Starting with the role, followed by the task, then the goal and then the example is better then doing it in reverse. What the exact best way of ordering a prompt is has not been found, however it is a important consideration to play with when prompt engineering.
\subsubsection*{Using explicit constraint}
Using explicit constraints in a prompt results in the LLM having to do less guess work and thus can generate better results that are in line with what you want. Some examples of things you can constrain are: which data to use, format of the output, scope, and length of the output.
\subsubsection*{Balancing user intent and model creativity}
Instructing everything to the last letter what the LLM has to do will most likely result in failure of the prompt. LLM's need a bit of space to what they are good at and to showcase their creative strength. Thus balancing the user intent and the model creativity is an important aspect of prompt engineering and one which should be kept in mind when trying to generate very specific results.


\subsection{Image generation model prompting}
The creating of prompts for image generative models is to and extent very similar to prompt engineering any LLM as they are both text based instruction models. Most of the aspects of making a good LLM prompt are transferable to image generation prompts, however there are some aspects unique to image generation which are discussed in this section.
\subsubsection*{Keywords}
One of the important aspects of a good image generative prompt is the usage of keywords. These are specific words which you do not place in the model, but are categories which the model uses in the background. Examples are subject, medium, style, resolution, color, and lightning. These are important categories for you to describe so that the model understands what you are looking to generate.
\subsubsection*{Negative prompt}
Image generative models have a system to allow the user to describe that which they do not want to see in the image. This is a great way to steer the image being generated as you can get more specific on the image you want to create from two different angles. Writing a negative prompt can be done in the same way one writes the primary prompt, however most of the time a negative prompt consists of styles and attributes such as disfigured deformed, ugly, and low resolution. But you can also do more specific things like saying magic if you are generating a wizard.
\subsubsection*{Association effect}
Models are build to link certain words with other words and thus specific words are associated to other words. This can have a large impact on the image you are generating as for example specificizing that you want someone with blue eyes will most likely result in the model generating a Europeans, Caucasian with blue eyes. While if instruct the model for brown eyes you will get a more diverse sample of people. This also happens with country names, celebrities, artists, and way more. This is an inherent bias that is inside the data used to train models and thus is part of the output as well. Being aware that this association effect exists and thus could impact the result of your generation is vital to generating images.
\subsubsection*{Custom models}
 Most image models are general models which generate anything the user asks of them, however if the user wants to generate a specific art style, pose, person, etc. without having to describe that they can use a fine-tuned models or fine-tune their own. Fine tuning allows a user to feed images that are similar to the result they are looking for into a model to train the model in what they should generate. For example a user could insert a certain comic book into a model to teach it to only create images that are similar to that art style. This is very beneficial if one wants to make many images with similar features or is looking to leverage models in more consistent ways. Examples of custom model types are checkpoint models, textual inversion, LoRA, LyCORIS and hypernetwork.
\subsubsection*{Using visual UI's}
 Almost all image generative models can be run from code using either an API call or running it locally on you computer, however there are also some visual user interfaces which can be used to generate images and these have many benefits. AUTOMATIC1111 is one of the best and most used image generation UI which is very powerful. It removes many of the issues models have and gives many handholds for the user to generate custom images. Loading in custom models, overcoming token limits, and more are all build in the system. However it can not be used to automate image generation (Outside of scripting) as that requires the usage of code. 
\subsubsection*{Image refiner}
Some model also have an extra model that can be used after generating the image. A so called refiner model which will clean up the generated image \cite{stabilityai_sdxl_refiner_1}. Make it sharper, more vibrant, up-scale the resolution, denoise the image, etc. This is an image to image model which can be used as an extra step, but is not required. It does take a lot longer than the generating of the image as it requires more function evaluations.

\section{Three primary forms of player interaction}
This section discusses the idea that TTRPGs have three primary forms of player interaction: \textbf{roleplay, exposition, and rule interaction.} The purpose of introducing this concept here is to establish a framework that can later be used to gather data in Chapter 6.  

\textbf{Roleplay} is where the players, including the Game Master, speak to each other while acting as their game characters, or in the case of the Game Master, as one or more NPCs. This kind of in-game interaction aligns closely with the narrativist aspect of GNS theory, where the goal is to create a story together \cite{edwards2001gns}. It is also referred to as \textit{in-character talk} and is one of the core communication styles during play \cite{bowman2010social, fine1983shared}.  

\textbf{Exposition} is when the Game Master narrates a moment in time, a location, or something else the players are experiencing in the game. The goal is to create a vivid image that helps the players immerse themselves in the world. Exposition sits between the narrativist and simulationist aspects of GNS theory and is generally classified as \textit{out-of-character speech}, since it describes situations rather than presenting dialogue between characters \cite{bowman2010social}. Its function is to construct a virtual reality in which the players can act, making exposition the key mechanism for creating these worlds \cite{ryan2001narrative}.  

\textbf{Rule interaction} is when the mechanical aspects of the game come into focus. Rolling dice, drawing cards, making checks, and using abilities are central to this form of interaction. By nature, it leans toward the gamist side of GNS theory, though depending on the system it can also overlap with narrativist or simulationist play. At its core, rule interaction is about using the system’s mechanics to overcome challenges. From a communication perspective, this fits into \textit{meta-talk}, though the boundary is often blurry \cite{bowman2010social}. Discussing rules, probabilities, or possible outcomes clearly falls under meta-talk, yet players may also minimize this by embedding mechanics into roleplay or exposition. This illustrates what Juul calls the \textit{half-real} nature of games: the interplay between rules and fiction, where meta-talk brings the game’s virtual reality to life \cite{juul2005half}.
